Therapeutic use

On this page

Research on tabletop RPGs (TTRPGs) keeps growing. Because You-Meet-In-A-Tavern (YMIAT) keeps math light and choices clear, it can be a practical system for guided or therapeutic play—when a trained facilitator runs the table. This page is a plain-language briefing first, then an academic literature review for readers who want the evidence map. It is not a clinical manual. Abbreviations and specialist terms are defined in the glossary.

Important: YMIAT is a game ruleset, not a medical product or certified treatment. Nothing here is medical advice, diagnosis, or a substitute for licensed care. Trauma, PTSD, and serious mental health work belong with qualified professionals. If you are in crisis, contact local emergency services or a crisis line in your country.

Why a light ruleset helps

Therapeutic and psychoeducational TTRPG work works best when players can stay present with the story and each other—not buried in exception stacks (Otani et al., 2024; Rosenblad et al., 2025). YMIAT is designed for that kind of presence:

  • Resolution stays on the d20—fewer dice rituals, faster feedback.
  • Ability modifiers only and a compressed Wounds scale keep bookkeeping short.
  • Spell Power, clear action costs, and optional Moments make risk and timing easy to talk about at the table.
  • Failure feeds Resolve, so setbacks can be framed as growth without needing a second rules chapter.

There is no peer-reviewed trial of “YMIAT therapy.” The point is fit: light rules reduce cognitive load so a facilitator can focus on safety, goals, and reflection—the same priorities frameworks for therapeutic TTRPGs emphasize (Otani et al., 2024).

What research suggests

The themes below summarize open literature and a Scholar scan for ttrpg roleplaying games d&d therapeutic use. Evidence is promising but often small-sample, qualitative, or early-stage—read it as direction, not proof. A fuller evidence map follows under Literature review.

Social skills and belonging

Guided TTRPGs give a low-stakes space to practice turn-taking, negotiation, and cooperative problem-solving. Structured programs treat the game as a delivery vehicle for social-skills and CBT-informed goals, not only as entertainment (Otani et al., 2024). Players also report community and emotional support as major benefits of regular play (Faul, 2025).

Emotion, anxiety, and mood

Case series and emerging clinical write-ups describe TTRPG groups used alongside care for anxiety, depression, identity exploration, autism support, and related concerns (Rosenblad et al., 2025). Results look encouraging for engagement and skill practice; larger controlled trials are still catching up. Treat outcomes as hopeful, not guaranteed.

Characters and self-concept

Bonds with player characters—treating a persona as a meaningful “second self”—are linked with clearer identity narratives, especially when the group feels socially safe (Snodgrass et al., 2026). Facilitation tip: invite reflection on why a character chose something, without forcing players to confess real-life parallels.

Trauma needs clinicians

Reviews note that RPG techniques can overlap with imaginal exposure, emotional regulation, and group process used in trauma care—but that work requires clinical training, consent, and pacing (Rami, 2025). Do not force trauma reenactment at a casual or educational table. YMIAT’s simplicity does not make unsupervised trauma work safe.

Facilitation is the method

Across sources, the Game Master / guide role matters as much as the system: session length, privacy, clear goals, adjustable challenge, and time to debrief (Otani et al., 2024; Rosenblad et al., 2025; Faul, 2025). A light ruleset helps—but it does not replace facilitation skill.

Practical tips for facilitators

Use these as a checklist when adapting YMIAT for psychoeducation, social skills groups, or clinician-led play.

  1. Name the frame. Say whether this is therapy, skills practice, or fun-first play with reflective moments. Align expectations before Session 1.
  2. Set goals in plain language. Examples: “practice asking for help,” “tolerate a failed roll,” “cooperate under time pressure.” Map scenes to one skill at a time (Otani et al., 2024).
  3. Consent and safety tools. Lines/veils, X-card or pause language, and the right to sit out a scene. Revisit after tough moments.
  4. Keep sessions short enough. Many interventional designs favor roughly CBT-session lengths (about 2–3 hours) rather than marathon combats that drain attention (Otani et al., 2024).
  5. Simplify the sheet further if needed. YMIAT already compresses math; for new players, pre-build characters and hide optional Moments until the group is ready.
  6. Use failure kindly. Missed rolls and Life Saves can model coping—or retraumatize if framed as humiliation. Narrate setbacks as information, then offer choices.
  7. Debrief every time. Five to ten minutes out-of-character: What felt hard? What did the group do well? What will we try next session?
  8. Protect privacy. Clinical notes stay in the clinical system—not in shared campaign docs.
  9. Know when to stop. Escalating distress, dissociation, or group harm means pause the fiction and use your professional protocol.

Limits and honest caveats

  • Much of the literature is qualitative, case-based, or small-N; generalizability is limited (Rosenblad et al., 2025; Faul, 2025).
  • Benefits depend heavily on group safety and culture (Snodgrass et al., 2026).
  • Some articles are open access; others may sit behind publisher access—use the DOI / abstract links below.
  • YMIAT remains a CC BY 4.0 fan homebrew. Cite the research; do not imply institutional endorsement of clinical claims.

Literature review

The following section is written for an academic reader. It is a narrative evidence review informed by secondary sources and citation chaining; it is not a formal dual-screener PRISMA systematic review.

Aim

This review maps peer-reviewed and adjacent empirical literature on tabletop role-playing games—especially Dungeons & Dragons and related analog systems—as adjunctive psychosocial or clinical interventions. The practical tip sections above treat YMIAT as a lightweight facilitation context. YMIAT itself has not been evaluated as a clinical intervention; the literature below concerns TTRPGs and therapeutically applied RPGs (TA-RPGs) in general.

Search strategy and inclusion criteria

The seed query was Google Scholar for ttrpg roleplaying games d&d therapeutic use (Scholar link). Screening was not limited to the first results page: related scoping and systematic reviews, rapid evidence assessments, and forward/backward citation chains were included. Priority was given to sources from 2000 to the present. Pre-2000 work appears only under Historical context, as historiography of pathology claims rather than contemporary efficacy evidence.

Include: peer-reviewed articles; scoping, systematic, and rapid evidence reviews; empirical theses with stable handles; open preprints clearly labeled as such when they add controlled-design information. Exclude: entertainment journalism without empirical grounding; clinical marketing claims without data. Language of the primary literature is predominantly English; this page summarizes in English (Dutch mirror available).

Limitations of this site review: single-author narrative synthesis without dual independent screening or a preregistered protocol; no formal risk-of-bias scoring across all primary studies; some full texts sit behind paywalls and were appraised via abstracts, open-access HTML/PDF, and secondary reviews.

Historical context (pre-2000)

Public controversy in the 1980s linked D&D to suicide and loss of reality testing—a moral panic later challenged by empirical work. Simon (1987) found no significant association between years of play and emotional stability on the 16PF among adolescent and adult players. Carter and Lester (1998) reported that D&D players did not differ from undergraduates on depression, suicidal ideation, psychoticism, extraversion, or neuroticism. Related comparisons similarly failed to support a withdrawn, clinically elevated “gamer personality” stereotype. These studies do not establish therapeutic benefit; they matter historically because they undercut the premise that tabletop fantasy play is inherently pathogenic—an assumption that shaped early clinical attitudes toward RPGs (Henrich & Worthington, 2021).

Evidence map: secondary sources

Secondary reviews form the spine of the contemporary field:

  • Arenas et al. (2022) scoped RPGs in mental health across 50 sources of evidence. Most work was therapeutic in intent (84%), often CBT-oriented (52%), and frequently targeted adolescents (70%). Formats split between computer (50%) and tabletop (44%) RPGs. Only about 16% of included studies were experimental—heterogeneity in definitions and outcomes precluded a meta-analytic systematic review.
  • Yuliawati et al. (2024) scoped TTRPG-as-psychological-intervention literature (2013–2023; 51 papers). Exploratory and qualitative designs predominated (n = 35); quantitative (n = 12) and mixed-method (n = 4) work remained minority. Geographic concentration in the United States, with scarce Asian samples, limits cultural generalizability. Reported potentials include cognitive/psychosocial skills, stress buffering, and adjunctive work with social anxiety, depressive symptoms, and autism-related needs.
  • Henrich and Worthington (2021) conducted a rapid evidence assessment of D&D specifically (13 studies through September 2020). Themes included absence of a unified maladaptive player personality, stakeholder attitudes, lack of evidence for maladaptive coping uniquely tied to D&D, and tentative psychological benefits (e.g., creativity, empathy, connectedness)—promising but preliminary.
  • Thompson and Majumder (2025) systematically reviewed TTRPGs for neurodiverse populations and identified only five eligible sources, overwhelmingly ASD-focused, with small samples and limited attention to ADHD and other neurodivergences.
  • Practice-facing syntheses and frameworks—e.g., Rosenblad et al. (2025) on clinical use of TTRPGs and Otani et al. (2024) on Critical Skills methodology—translate the sparse experimental base into facilitation guidance rather than definitive efficacy claims.

Outcome domains

Social skills, connectedness, and group process

Across reviews, cooperative problem-solving, turn-taking, and low-stakes rehearsal of interpersonal scripts are the most consistently reported targets (Arenas et al., 2022; Yuliawati et al., 2024). Abbott et al. (2022) described a year-long D&D-informed group for adults aimed at social connectedness; participants reported greater confidence in social situations and transfer of practiced skills to everyday contexts. Baker et al. (2023) situate such findings within a broader call for RPGs as mental-health adjuncts while noting the thin experimental base. Qualitative and thesis work similarly foregrounds community and emotional support as player-reported benefits of ongoing play (Faul, 2025).

Anxiety, depression, and related mood outcomes

Clinical case series and program descriptions report TTRPG groups used alongside care for anxiety and depression (Rosenblad et al., 2025). Controlled evidence remains scarce. A waitlist-controlled, CBT-infused D&D TA-RPG trial in Australian adult community mental health (N = 33; preprint) reported within-group reductions in social anxiety and depression and increased self-esteem relative to baseline for the intervention arm, without corresponding waitlist changes; between-group interaction patterns and PTSD outcomes warrant cautious reading pending peer review (Rae et al., 2025). Computerized RPG-like CBT packages (e.g., SPARX) contribute higher-quality adolescent depression evidence but lack the face-to-face social exposure inherent to tabletop groups—an important construct distinction when interpreting “RPG therapy” claims (Arenas et al., 2022).

Autism and neurodiversity

Neurodiversity-focused synthesis is promising but thin and ASD-skewed (Thompson & Majumder, 2025). Atherton et al. (2024) used short-form online D&D with autistic adults (N = 8) and reported a safer social environment, reduced masking pressure, kinship, and identity-related “bleed” between character and self. Henning et al. (2024) evaluated social-skills training via tabletop RPG sessions for six autistic adolescents across in-person and pandemic-online phases; in-person phases showed more favorable social-skills inventory patterns than online continuation, underscoring modality as a confound. Overall, evidence supports TTRPGs as structured social practice contexts more strongly than as proven standalone treatments.

Identity, character bonding, and self-concept

Snodgrass et al. (2026) frame tabletop play as “drama therapy in the wild,” linking personal bonds with characters to improved self-concept when social safety is present. Atherton et al. (2024) similarly describe character play as a vehicle for trying on agency. These findings are largely observational or qualitative; causal pathways (identification, narrative coherence, group mirroring) remain hypothesized.

Trauma and PTSD

Rami (2025) reviews overlaps between RPG techniques and trauma-informed processes (imaginal exposure, emotion regulation, group containment) while stressing that trauma work requires clinical training, pacing, and consent. Unstructured “reenactment” of personal trauma at recreational tables is contraindicated. The present review treats trauma applications as clinician-only adjuncts, not homebrew DIY protocols.

Methodological appraisal

The corpus is dominated by qualitative interviews, case reports, small pilots, and scoping reviews (Arenas et al., 2022; Yuliawati et al., 2024). Threats to inference include: (a) selection and expectancy bias among self-selecting hobbyists and motivated clinicians; (b) facilitator skill as an uncontrolled active ingredient; (c) heterogeneous definitions of “RPG” spanning tabletop, computer, LARP, and MMO formats; (d) nonstandardized outcomes and short follow-up; (e) WEIRD and adolescent overrepresentation; (f) publication bias favoring positive narrative reports. Where controlled designs exist, samples are often underpowered for between-group interactions, and manuals are rarely publicly standardized.

Proposed mechanisms and facilitation

Recurring mechanistic accounts include structured social rehearsal; narrative identity work and character–player bleed; self-determination needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness); CBT skill practice embedded in fiction; and classic group-therapy processes such as cohesion and interpersonal learning (Otani et al., 2024; Rosenblad et al., 2025; Snodgrass et al., 2026). Facilitation—session length, consent tools, adjustable challenge, debrief—appears as consequential as system choice. Lightweight rulesets are theoretically attractive for reducing cognitive load and keeping attention on interpersonal and reflective goals, but system comparisons (rules-light vs. rules-heavy) remain largely untested.

Gaps and implications

Priority gaps include adequately powered randomized and waitlist-controlled trials of manualized TA-TTRPG protocols; ADHD and non-ASD neurodiversity samples; non-Western and collectivist cultural settings (Yuliawati et al., 2024); longer follow-up; and transparent reporting of adverse events and emotional bleed. Until that evidence matures, TTRPGs are best framed as facilitator-dependent adjuncts—plausible for social practice, engagement, and identity exploration; not established standalone treatments. For YMIAT users, the implication is pragmatic rather than clinical: a light ruleset may free attentional resources for the facilitation practices the literature already treats as central.

Glossary

Hover or focus a dotted term for a short definition; click to jump here. Entries are alphabetical by abbreviation or headword.

16PF
Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire—a psychometric inventory of personality traits used in some older D&D player studies.
ADHD
Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder.
Adjunct / adjunctive
Used alongside usual care or another treatment—not as a proven standalone therapy.
ASD
Autism spectrum disorder.
Bleed
In role-play research: emotional or identity spillover between player and character (and sometimes the reverse).
CBT
Cognitive behavioural therapy—a structured psychological approach targeting thoughts, feelings, and behaviours.
D&D
Dungeons & Dragons—the best-known commercial tabletop role-playing game system.
LARP
Live-action role-playing—physical enactment of characters, distinct from tabletop play.
MMO / MMORPG
Massively multiplayer online (role-playing) game—networked digital RPG, not tabletop.
PRISMA
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses—reporting standards for formal systematic reviews (this page does not claim a full PRISMA review).
PTSD
Post-traumatic stress disorder.
REA
Rapid evidence assessment—a structured, faster-than-full-systematic synthesis of available studies.
RPG
Role-playing game—broad category that can include tabletop, computer, live-action, and online forms.
Scoping review
A review that maps the breadth of literature on a topic rather than estimating a single pooled effect size.
Self-determination (SDT)
Self-determination theory: psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness often invoked as mechanisms in game-based interventions.
SPARX
A computerized, fantasy-themed CBT programme for adolescent depression—related to “RPG therapy” claims but not a face-to-face tabletop group.
TA-RPG / TA-TTRPG
Therapeutically applied (tabletop) role-playing game—play deliberately structured by a clinician or trained facilitator toward therapeutic or psychoeducational goals.
TTRPG
Tabletop role-playing game—shared storytelling with rules, typically face-to-face or online with a game master and player characters.
Waitlist-controlled
A study design comparing people who receive the intervention now with people who wait and receive usual care until later.
WEIRD
Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic—shorthand for samples that overrepresent those societies and limit cultural generalizability.
YMIAT
You-Meet-In-A-Tavern—this site’s lightweight ruleset; not itself a validated clinical product.
